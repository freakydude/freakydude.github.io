Why I Prefer Arch Linux

The pros and cons of Arch Linux compared to other popular Linux distributions, and why it is my favorite.
April 22, 2021 · 3 min · freakyDude |  Suggest Changes
Table of Contents
  • Widely used distributions
  • Conclusion

I will start a series of posts about configuring Arch Linux in a slim, straightforward way. But let me begin with some pros and cons of the Arch Linux distribution.

Update (November 2026): I originally claimed that both Slackware and Gentoo are rolling releases like Arch Linux. That is only true for Gentoo; Slackware uses stable releases, and the section below is corrected.

The Arch Linux distribution describes itself as “a lightweight and flexible Linux distribution that tries to Keep It Simple”.

That’s it: Arch Linux is absolutely the right distribution for you if you like a system that only does what you have installed and configured. It does it only that way, and no magic happens. In addition, it has a great community with an up-to-date wiki about nearly all aspects of the system. So if you have to configure a package, it’s mostly enough to follow the associated article there.

Widely used distributions#

There is no need to dive in as deeply as you have to with Slackware or Gentoo. For example, normally you don’t have to compile anything, but you have the freedom to do it anyway. Like Arch Linux, Gentoo is a rolling release. Slackware is not: it has stable point releases (version 15.0 came out in February 2022), plus a -current development branch that is not meant for production systems. With a rolling release, you can always have the latest stable version of the kernel and all software packages, mostly on the same day of their release. That’s a big pro for update junkies, and you never have to do a big upgrade after half a year of waiting.

On the other hand, there are Debian-based distributions like Ubuntu. They work out of the box. They have a graphical installer, pre-configured packages, and proprietary drivers for, e.g., graphics cards. Most Debian-based distributions are not rolling releases. They have big releases from time to time, and you can choose to upgrade or stay with your release for a while. Updates are mostly security fixes only. If you are a beginner or just want a good-looking Linux without having to worry about internals, choose one of them.

Conclusion#

In my opinion, Arch Linux hits the sweet spot. Without spending most of your time configuring details, you get an always up-to-date, slim distribution. And while working with it, you have the chance to learn a lot about how the Linux ecosystem works.

In future posts, I will help you with some aspects of it.
